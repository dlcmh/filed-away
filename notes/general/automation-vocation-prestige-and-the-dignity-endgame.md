# Automation, vocation prestige, and the dignity endgame

Notes on why the recurring panic about automation destroying jobs misprices
them, what the software-engineering version of the panic gets right this time,
and what an endgame of affordable dignity would actually require.

## The anxiety is priced at former prestige, not actual job content

Factory work was a prestige vocation for one generation — stable, pensioned,
one income sufficient for a house and upward mobility for the children — and a
cautionary tale for the next. The parents mourning it had spent thirty years
telling their own children to study hard precisely so they would not end up on
the line. Automation anxiety is always priced at the vocation's former
prestige while the actual job content had already become repetitive,
surveilled, and ergonomically punishing. The worry is about losing jobs that
parents were already steering children away from.

The correct comparison is not "AI takes the coding jobs" but "what did those
jobs do to the humans while they existed." A profession built on 996 hours,
age-35 exit ramps, and burnout-as-culture was already consuming its workforce
and discarding them young. Automation is not destroying a golden vocation; it
is ending a bargain that had already gone bad — high pay in exchange for your
thirties.

## Software engineering: parallel, with one wrinkle

The parallel part: hand-writing cross-platform interfaces was never the
prestige substance of the work — it was the toll booth. The substance was
judgment: what to build, which trade-offs serve the business, what the user
needs versus asked for. Every abstraction layer — assemblers, compilers,
garbage collection, frameworks — ate drudgery, and demand for software
expanded to absorb the displaced effort. The ladder keeps getting shorter at
the bottom and taller at the top.

The wrinkle: all previous abstractions *widened* the profession. AI is the
first that plausibly *narrows* it, because it automates the
judgment-adjacent middle too, and because demand may finally saturate — there
is only so much software a society metabolizes. The layoffs read differently:
not the usual churn of skills becoming obsolete, but the early contour of the
profession shrinking toward those who direct machines rather than operate
them. Whether that fraction is 5% or 50% is genuinely unknown.

## The unresolved question is distributional

The vocation dying is not the tragedy; the vocation dying *without its former
occupants capturing any of the efficiency they trained for* is. The factory
automation wave answered badly — productivity decoupled from wages around
precisely that era. Nothing about the AI wave inherently answers better. That
is a policy and ownership question, not a technical one, and it is the same in
every country.

## The dignity endgame, and its two mechanisms

If AI and robotics drive the marginal cost of manufactured goods,
construction, and services toward the cost of energy plus compute, the
society that wins is not the one with the most jobs but the one that converts
automation surplus into the lowest *required* cost of living. Two mechanisms:

1. **Deflation of the survival basket** (energy, transport, housing, food), so
   the income needed for dignity shrinks. China's structural advantages:
   state capacity to build housing and energy at scale; an industrial base
   that will automate aggressively precisely because labor is becoming scarce
   and expensive (the demographic cliff forces it); a policy vocabulary
   ("common prosperity") that already names redistribution, whatever its
   uneven execution.
2. **A large mobile middle class with leisure spending** — the micro-scale
   version operating today in Dali Old City: buskers earning a living in
   restaurant-bars because rent is a fraction of Kunming's and a decade of
   rural roads and rail made the town reachable. The busker is the smallest
   unit of the endgame: a self-chosen field of work, locally sustainable, with
   no age-35 exit ramp and a gentle depreciation curve — a repertoire and a
   voice age better than a web-framework skill set. (The C-drama 去有风的地方
   dramatized exactly this: the burned-out metro professional defecting to a
   Yunnan town where the plot is, gloriously, nothing.)

Two limits on the endgame. First, who owns the automated capital base
determines who the deflation benefits; material security without individual
autonomy — "decide what you would rather do" granted as a ration rather than
a right — is the deep political tension, not an economic one. Second, the
order of operations: China is attempting the transition at ~$13k GDP per
capita where the comparable social guarantees elsewhere were attempted at
multiples of that; a generation may be squeezed between — too old to retrain,
too young to have accumulated, arriving exactly when the drudgery jobs that
would have employed them vanish.

## The middle-power variant

A middle power (Malaysia in semiconductors: Penang's fifty years of back-end
assembly and test) cannot deflation-engineer post-scarcity alone — it lacks
scale in energy, compute, and capital. Its path runs through *position*
rather than *abundance*: irreplaceable niches (advanced packaging, equipment
components, design services) where human judgment stays scarce and defensibly
paid, while the giants' automation drives down the cost of imported goods. A
free-rider variant of the same endgame — viable to the extent the niche holds.
