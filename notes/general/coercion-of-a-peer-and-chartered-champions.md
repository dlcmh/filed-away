# Coercion of a peer, and chartered champions

Notes on why Eight-Nation-Alliance-style coercion of China cannot be repeated,
what the soft-siege successor can and cannot do, and why a deindustrializing
West may drift toward chartered champions on the East India Company pattern.

## Why the 1900 pattern is foreclosed

The Eight-Nation Alliance (1900) was possible because the price of coercion
was bounded — an agrarian, internally collapsing Qing state could not
manufacture rifles or warships at scale, and eight industrial powers could
project force into it at the cost of casualties, not existence. Every condition
has inverted:

1. **The nuclear fact dominates.** Any invasion-scale attack on Chinese
   homeland territory escalates against a nuclear-armed state with survivable
   second-strike forces. No coalition can wage expeditionary war against a
   peer nuclear power without accepting the possibility of losing cities, and
   every member with a veto over the coalition knows it.
2. **Industrial symmetry inverted.** The would-be coercers are the
   deindustrializing ones: US submarine construction takes years at rate;
   European munitions stockpiles proved insufficient within months of a land
   war on Europe's own continent; the shipbuilding tonnage gap versus China
   is on the order of 200:1. An expeditionary war against China's coastline
   means fighting the country that builds most of the world's ships and
   drones, at the end of a 10,000-km logistics line, inside the defender's
   missile envelope. (South Korea and Japan are the real exceptions to
   Western deindustrialization; one or two countries do not make an alliance,
   and Seoul's appetite for an anti-China war is negligible.)
3. **The coalition cannot be assembled.** The 1900 members had no
   entanglements with the target. China is the largest trading partner of well
   over a hundred countries, including most US treaty allies. Every member of
   a hypothetical coalition would be voting to devastate its own economy to a
   degree no domestic polity would sustain.
4. **The target learned the lesson specifically.** The entire post-humiliation
   architecture — nuclear forces, the world's densest air-defense and
   anti-ship environment, food and energy stockpiles, indigenous chip
   programs — exists because the carved-melon cartoon is in the national
   memory. One cannot repeat a coercion against a state whose institutional
   identity is the prevention of that coercion, armed with the deterrent that
   did not exist the first time.

## The successor: siege, not invasion

The export-control regime, entity lists, and chip-tool embargoes are the same
coercive family by other means. On current evidence, the soft siege imposes
costs, redirects trajectories, and unites the target — it accelerated
indigenous substitution (the Big Fund vindication) more than it changed
policy. A naval blockade in a Taiwan contingency is the hypothetical where
coalition coercion turns physical again; even there, blockade is an act of war
against a nuclear state whose trade the blockading nations depend on, across
a line saturated with anti-ship missiles — a mutual suicide vest. The tragedy
of coercing China was always that it worked once.

## Chartered champions: from the East India Company to SpaceX

The East India Company was not a company that did some colonizing — it *was*
the colonial state: a private army larger than Britain's, taxation of
millions, treaty-making as a sovereign, until the 1857 rebellion forced
nationalization. The structural condition: a state wanting expansion into an
arena too distant to administer cost-effectively *franchised sovereignty* to a
joint-stock company.

SpaceX is the closest modern analog, with three EIC-grade traits already
visible: it owns strategic infrastructure the state depends on (all of NASA's
human spaceflight, much national-security launch; Starlink as battlefield
infrastructure in Ukraine, with the company deciding service geography); it
builds sovereign-adjacent geography (Starbase as an incorporated company town;
publicly floated self-governance principles for Mars settlements); and it
operates under charter economics — sole-source anchor contracts, regulatory
forbearance, a moat the state protects because it cannot afford the
alternative.

The decisive near-term counterweight: the modern state is not weak *at home*.
It can nationalize, regulate, or break the company with a stroke; the capital
base sits inside US jurisdiction and export-control law. Today's SpaceX holds
delegated power — an instrument of the state — the EIC inverted.

The transition becomes plausible when the arena outruns the state's reach:
Mars, orbital industry at scale, asteroid resources, or jurisdiction-escaping
compute. Given a deindustrialized capacity to build state alternatives
directly, the US franchising orbit to a champion is the historically cheap
option, and the precedent (cost-plus, revolving doors, forbearance) is set.
The pattern: a VOC-at-sea — a chartered monopoly doing the state's work at
the state's pleasure — becomes an EIC when the "at the state's pleasure"
clause stops being enforceable. Nobody notices the transition until the
company controls the transport monopoly and the settlement, and the state
discovers its empire is rented. Timeline: decades. Direction of travel:
plausible.
